Old Quarter Scams in Hanoi: What to Watch For (and How to Avoid Them)

By Carl Tomich. Published 2026-01-09; updated 11 September 2026.

Home / Blog / Old Quarter Scams in Hanoi: What to Watch For (and How to Avoid Them)

By Carl Tomich | Updated September 11, 2026 | Editorial policy

The Old Quarter is the heart of Hanoi, but it is also where tourists are most likely to be overcharged. Here is how to enjoy the energy, eat well, and avoid the common traps.

Published from Hanoi Updated 2025

Why the Old Quarter Feels Like a Movie Set

Hanoi’s Old Quarter is only a few square kilometers, yet it packs in more life than some entire cities. The streets follow a centuries-old layout built around trade guilds. That is why you still see names like Hang Bac (silver) or Hang Gai (silk). Every block is a mix of scooters, food stalls, temples, cafés, and colonial-era buildings squeezed into an environment that feels more like a living market than a tidy tourism district.

Busy visitor areas can create pressure to make purchases quickly, but there is no reliable basis here for ranking the Old Quarter against the rest of Vietnam. The useful approach is to recognise unclear terms and pause before accepting a service.

A Quick Primer on How the Old Quarter Works

The Old Quarter has narrow streets and busy pavements. Use crossings where practical, watch for turning traffic and choose a route you feel comfortable walking. Do not assume vehicles will yield simply because you keep moving.

Most scams in the Old Quarter are built around momentum. A vendor starts talking before you have time to ask the price. A stranger offers to help or guide you. A friendly smile and constant motion makes it feel rude to pause. The best defense is simple: stop the momentum. Ask for a price before accepting a service, confirm it again, and walk away if anything feels vague.

The Core Old Quarter Scams (and How They Work)

A common consumer problem is a price that changes after a service has begun. The examples below describe patterns to watch for, not a claim that every vendor or driver uses them. If a dispute becomes threatening, prioritise leaving safely.

  • Overpriced cyclo rides: Cyclos are iconic and slow-moving, which makes them a tourist magnet. The scam happens when the driver says a low price for a short loop, then demands a higher rate per person, per hour, or for a “tip.” Always agree on a total price in Vietnamese dong before you sit down, confirm if it is per person, and have the exact cash ready. If the price feels too cheap to be real, it probably is.
  • Shoe cleaning or repair hustle: Someone points at your shoes, gestures that they are dirty, and starts cleaning without clear permission. Then they ask for a high payment or insist on extra repairs. The simplest response is a firm “No thanks” and keep moving. If you actually want a clean, ask for the price upfront and pay only what you agreed.
  • Beer Street price swaps: On Ta Hien (Beer Street), menus can change or drinks can be brought at a different price from what you saw. Keep the menu, take a quick photo if needed, and confirm prices before ordering. If you are in a group, nominate one person to handle the order and bill to avoid confusion.
  • Taxi and ride booking: Use a recognised booking service such as Grab or Be where available, or a clearly identified licensed taxi. Check the vehicle and driver against your booking, and keep the trip record. No app removes every risk.
  • Photo fee surprise: Some vendors offer to place a basket on your shoulder or pose with a street setup, then demand a fee for the photo. If you want the shot, ask the price first. If not, a polite wave and step back avoids the awkwardness.
  • Friendly guide detours: Someone offers to show you a temple or a shortcut, then leads you to a shop with inflated prices. Unless it is a licensed tour you booked, decline and stick to your plan. The Old Quarter is dense; you do not need a guide to find food or a lake walk.

Common Scams Across Vietnam (Not Just Hanoi)

The Old Quarter gets the attention, but the broader Vietnam scam list is consistent from Hanoi to Ho Chi Minh City, Hoi An, and Nha Trang. The tricks do not change much; only the setting does. Knowing the wider patterns helps you relax on the rest of your trip.

Motorbike Rental Extras

Some rental shops advertise a low daily rate and then add fees for helmets, scratches, or “late return.” Take photos of the bike from every angle, confirm the return time, and keep a copy of your agreement. If a shop refuses to document the bike’s condition, choose a different one.

Overpriced Taxi to Airport

Airport transfers are a classic target for inflated prices, especially if you look like you just landed. Use ride-hailing apps or pre-book a hotel transfer with a clear price in writing. If you do take a taxi, insist on the meter and follow the route on your map app.

Menu Switches and Service Fees

A menu shows one price, but the bill shows another with extra charges. This happens in tourist-heavy areas. Ask if service charge or taxes are included before ordering. Keep the menu close and do not be afraid to request a correction before paying.

Currency Confusion

Vietnam has large denominations, and it is easy to mix up a 20,000 note with a 200,000 note. Count your cash, keep smaller notes accessible, and pay slowly. If a vendor says you gave them the wrong amount, stay calm and recount together.

Fake Police Shakedowns

Unclear official demands: If someone claiming authority requests money and you are uncertain, stay calm and seek verification through an official channel. Ask for help from your accommodation or consular assistance when needed; do not rely on a blanket rule about uniforms or fine collection.

Street Drugs on Beer Street

Unknown substances: Decline street offers of drugs or unknown substances. Do not assume a product is legal or safe because it is openly offered. Consult current official travel advice for local-law information.

How to Move Through the Old Quarter Without Stress

The most effective scam prevention is not confrontation; it is quiet confidence. Take time to make purchasing decisions, and keep your body language neutral. If someone tries to engage you with a service you did not ask for, a smile and “No thanks” is enough. You can decline an unwanted service without debating it.

That said, you can still be friendly. Vietnam is warm and hospitable, and the majority of people you meet are just trying to make a living, not scam you. The trick is to separate hospitality from sales. If someone is offering a service, treat it like a business interaction: confirm the price, confirm what is included, and pay what you agreed to.

Practical Tips That Actually Work

  • Use a recognised transport app where available, check the displayed fare and payment method, and retain the trip record.
  • Carry small notes. It avoids the “no change” trick and makes quick payments easier.
  • Ask “How much total?” instead of “How much?” to avoid per-person surprises.
  • Check when payment is due before agreeing. Deposits and advance tickets can be legitimate; verify the seller and terms instead of treating every advance payment as a scam.
  • Use Google Translate or a price calculator app if a negotiation feels stuck.
  • Screenshot menus or storefront signs if you are unsure about pricing.

The Old Quarter Is Still Worth It

It is easy to let scam warnings turn into paranoia. Do not let that happen. The Old Quarter is one of the most photogenic, delicious, and energetic neighborhoods in Southeast Asia. If you learn a few basics, you can enjoy it without stress. Start your mornings with a slow walk around Hoan Kiem Lake, grab a coffee on a quiet side street, and save Beer Street for a short, controlled visit if you want the nightlife.

Treat the Old Quarter as a working neighbourhood. Order clearly, be considerate when photographing people, and decline unwanted services without guilt. At busy roads, take the time to find a suitable crossing rather than relying on confidence alone.

A manageable Old Quarter walking plan

If you want a simple route that avoids the worst pressure points, start at sunrise around Hoan Kiem Lake. Head north along Hang Be and Hang Bac when shops are opening. Grab breakfast at a small pho or bun cha spot where locals are eating. Midday, duck into a café on a quieter street such as Bao Khanh. In the late afternoon, walk west toward St. Joseph’s Cathedral for golden light and fewer crowds.

If you want to see Beer Street, choose a time and venue that feel comfortable to you. Crowding can make it harder to keep the group together or check an order. Leave when you have had enough rather than staying because an itinerary says you should.

Final Thoughts: Respect the Hustle, Protect Your Trip

A bad transaction should be assessed on what happened, without turning it into a claim about an entire country or the motives of people working there. Clear terms, calm questions and a record of the purchase help protect the rest of your trip.

If you do get overcharged, treat it as an expensive lesson and move on. The worst thing you can do is let one small moment ruin your entire perception of the country. Keep your standards, keep your calm, and enjoy the Old Quarter for what it is: a crowded, unforgettable slice of Hanoi that still feels alive even after midnight.

Scam Red Flags

  • Someone starts a service without asking.
  • Pricing is vague or changes mid-way.
  • You are rushed to decide or pay.
  • The bill shows items you did not order.
  • A “too good to be true” offer appears.

Where to Reset

If the Old Quarter feels intense, reset with a lake walk, a café break, or a short ride to Tay Ho. You will return calmer and clearer.

A higher price is not always a scam

Separate deliberate deception from an expensive offer, a misunderstanding or a service you simply do not want. A café can charge more than the stall next door without cheating anyone. A cyclo ride can cost more than a car journey because you are buying a different experience. The important questions are whether the total was clear, whether you agreed to it and whether the service matched that agreement.

This distinction helps you respond proportionately. If the displayed price is more than you want to pay, decline before ordering. If a bill includes something you did not receive, ask for that item to be explained or removed. If the disagreement involves a translation problem, use a calculator or written message. Starting with an accusation can turn an ordinary correction into a confrontation that neither side needed.

Avoid treating every friendly approach as a warning sign. Some people are selling something, some are offering help and some are simply being sociable. You can be courteous without accepting a purchase or handing over your belongings. A short refusal, followed by continuing with your day, is usually clearer than a long explanation of why you do not need the service.

Agree on four things before a cyclo ride

First establish the route. “Around the lake” and “around the Old Quarter” can mean different distances and stopping patterns. Show the end point on a map if necessary. Next establish the duration, including whether photo stops count. Then confirm the number of passengers covered. Finally, write the total in Vietnamese dong and check that both sides understand the same number.

This is a hypothetical example, not a local price recommendation: if a quotation says 200,000, ask whether that is for the whole ride or for each passenger. The extra question is much more useful than arguing later about what “two hundred” meant. Take a screenshot of a written quotation if the arrangement is made by message. Keep enough suitable cash accessible without displaying your whole wallet.

A driver who will not clarify the basic terms is someone you can politely decline before boarding. Once the ride begins, tell them promptly if the route appears different from what you agreed. There may be a road closure or another ordinary explanation. Ask before assuming the detour is dishonest, and avoid distracting someone while they are negotiating traffic.

At the table: order so the bill is easy to check

Read the menu before settling in, especially where staff are actively drawing people from the street. Check the unit as well as the number: a price can be per dish, per portion, per weight or per person. If an item has no visible price, ask before ordering. For seafood or anything sold by weight, establish the quantity and estimated total while you can still decide against it.

Groups create room for accidental confusion. One person can keep a simple note of the order and another can check that delivered items belong to your table. Ask whether an unsolicited snack or opened drink is included before consuming it. Keep track of rounds rather than reconstructing the evening from memory at the end. This is ordinary bill management, not a declaration that the venue is untrustworthy.

If the amount seems wrong, ask to see the itemised bill and point to the specific line. “We ordered two of these” is easier to resolve than “This whole bill is a scam.” Keep your voice low and give staff a chance to correct a mistake. If the situation becomes threatening, put your safety ahead of recovering a small sum. You can document the dispute and seek help after leaving the immediate pressure.

Transport apps reduce uncertainty, but still require attention

Match the driver and vehicle to the booking, and confirm the destination before setting off. Do not get into a different car because someone nearby says they are helping the assigned driver. If the driver asks you to cancel the app booking and pay privately, you lose part of the record that made the booking useful. Resolve changes through the platform where possible.

Read the displayed payment method so you do not accidentally pay cash for a ride already charged electronically. Some legitimate charges may be treated separately under the service's terms, so check the app's explanation rather than assuming every adjustment is fraud. If you dispute a charge, retain the trip record and use the platform's support channel. A screenshot can help if your connection becomes unreliable.

For airport arrivals, use a clearly identified official taxi arrangement, a confirmed hotel transfer or a ride booked through a recognised service. Smartraveller recommends these approaches in its Vietnam guidance. Avoid letting an unsolicited helper take your phone and organise an unknown vehicle on your behalf. Official Vietnam travel advice.

Keep a small record if something goes wrong

Write down the time, location, business name and amount while you still remember them. Save the booking, receipt, messages and any photograph you already took of a menu or vehicle. Do not put yourself at risk trying to obtain evidence from someone who is angry. A factual record made afterwards can still be useful to a booking platform, bank, insurer or police officer.

For a disputed card charge, contact your card provider through its official app or the number on the card. Explain what happened and ask about the appropriate process; do not assume every disagreement qualifies for a reversal. If the card or phone has been stolen, securing accounts takes priority over writing a public review. Use another trusted device if needed and avoid sharing verification codes with anyone who contacts you unexpectedly.

If someone claiming to be an official demands payment and you are unsure what is happening, remain calm and seek verification through an official channel. Ask for identification and an explanation where it is safe to do so. Contact your accommodation or consular assistance if you need help communicating. A travel blog cannot reliably identify a real officer by uniform or by a blanket claim about how every fine must be collected.

Protect the rest of the evening

Before going out, save your accommodation address in Vietnamese, charge your phone and decide how you will get home. Keep a backup payment method separate from the wallet you use for small purchases. If travelling with others, agree on a meeting point in case phones fail or the group separates. These small arrangements matter more once the streets are crowded and everyone is tired.

Choose somewhere to stop before using your phone near traffic. Navigating while walking off the pavement divides your attention between a screen, vehicles and people. Use a crossing where practical and wait for a suitable opportunity; no walking technique guarantees that traffic will give way. If a particular road feels beyond your comfort, take a different route or ask for help from your accommodation.

The goal is to leave attention available for Hanoi itself. You should be able to enjoy a bowl of noodles, a shopfront or the noise around Ta Hien without conducting a running investigation. Make the commercial details clear, keep control of your belongings and step away from pressure.
