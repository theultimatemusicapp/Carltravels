What It Really Costs to Live in Hanoi as a Digital Nomad

I swapped Australia for Vietnam and spent my first month in Hanoi's Tay Ho district. Here are the real numbers on rent, food, transport, and lifestyle—plus how to make friends fast.

Watch the full video (16 mins) Published from Hanoi

Quick Budget Snapshot

  • Airbnb studio in Tay Ho with kitchenette and lake view: $320 USD/month. Local apartments start around $150.
  • Food: phở for $1.50, banh mi for $1, daily spend on meals and drinks about $10–20 AUD.
  • Transport: Grab motorbike rides to the CBD for ~$1. Sleeper buses and trains keep intercity trips cheap.
  • Gyms: memberships from $38 AUD/month, plus Muay Thai and jujitsu classes available nearby.

Why Hanoi (and Why Now)

After two months in Japan, I moved to Vietnam for a lower cost of living, vibrant culture, and a growing digital nomad scene. Tay Ho feels cosmopolitan but still local, and it keeps me close to friends and collaborators while I prep for a Da Nang trip—the “new Bali” for remote workers.

Accommodation

My current Airbnb studio runs $320 USD/month with a queen bed, kitchenette, and a slice of lake view. If you’re willing to sign a local lease, you can find simple apartments from $150 USD in Tay Ho and across the river. Utilities are low and most places come furnished.

Food & Daily Costs

Meals rarely crack $3. A bowl of phở is $1.50, banh mi is a single dollar, and coffee shops abound. I spend $10–20 AUD per day covering food, transport, and the occasional drink—far less than the $19 banh mi shocker back in Australia.

Getting Around

Grab (Uber for Southeast Asia) is the go-to: a $1 ride gets me to the CBD. Night buses and sleeper trains make longer hops comfortable and cheap, and the new metro keeps expanding. Sidewalks can be chaotic—watch your step and keep a mask handy on smoggier days.

Community & Work

Meeting people is easy if you show up. Facebook groups like “Hanoi Massive” and expat meetups around West Lake fill the calendar. English teaching remains a solid USD income stream, and digital nomads mix coworking with café hopping for Wi‑Fi and views.

Pros, Cons, and What’s Next

Hanoi’s affordability, street food scene, and social energy make it easy to settle in. Pollution spikes and broken sidewalks are the trade-offs, but they’re manageable with masks and good shoes. Next up: a scouting trip to Da Nang to see if it really is the new Bali for remote workers.
