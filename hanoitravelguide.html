Hanoi Travel Guide: Three Days to One Week

By Carl Tomich | Published 2025-04-25 | Updated 11 September 2026

A practical Hanoi itinerary covering neighbourhoods, food, transport, day trips, and what to avoid.

Home / Destinations / Hanoi

Give Hanoi three unhurried days for the city itself, or roughly a week if you want to add an overnight bay trip and keep a little breathing room. Stay near the Old Quarter for a first visit focused on walking, food and central sights. Consider Tay Ho when a quieter daily routine matters more than being able to walk to everything on your list.

The useful decision is how much movement you want in each day. Hanoi can become exhausting when the plan sends you across town repeatedly, then adds a late night and an early tour pickup. This itinerary groups activities by area, leaves room for weather and avoids making access to a railway attraction the centre of the trip.

Carl’s films from Hanoi, Vietnamese lesson testimonial and guides to neighbourhood life and northern excursions are linked below. The recordings document earlier experiences; use the written planning advice alongside current information from the places you intend to visit. Practical planning information was reviewed on 11 September 2026; confirm current opening hours, tickets and transport with the operator before paying.

Choose your base around your actual days

The Old Quarter and Hoan Kiem

For a short first visit, start your accommodation search around the Old Quarter and Hoan Kiem Lake. Vietnam Tourism’s Old Quarter guide gives a useful introduction to its streets, shops and food. The planning advantage is simple: several outings can begin on foot, and you can return to your room without turning every break into another cross-city journey.

Central does not necessarily mean quiet. Read recent reviews for street noise, building work, lifts and the location of the actual room. A hotel photograph of a window does not tell you whether your booked category has one. Ask specifically about the room you intend to reserve, particularly if sleep, daylight or step-free access will affect your stay.

Tay Ho and West Lake

Tay Ho is worth considering for a longer stay built around work, exercise or repeated visits to the same cafés. Carl’s existing Hanoi material has a strong focus on this area. The compromise is transport: check the journey from the exact accommodation to the places you expect to visit, rather than treating the whole lakeside area as one compact neighbourhood.

Do not book a month simply because an apartment looks good online. For a longer stay, start with a shorter booking, inspect the building and test the routine you actually need. The Hanoi living-cost article and Tay Ho café guide provide further context, but current rent and service terms need checking directly.

Make arrival day easy to recover from

Save your accommodation name, street address and contact details offline before flying. Check the booking's arrival instructions, especially for a late landing. If you arrange a transfer, confirm the meeting point and what happens after a delayed flight. A clear pickup agreement is more useful than discovering at arrivals that “airport transfer available” meant you needed to book it separately.

Compare a prearranged transfer, an official airport taxi and a ride booked through an established app using current quotes. Check the vehicle, destination, currency, tolls and any airport surcharge. The Australian Government’s Vietnam travel advice recommends official or pre-booked airport transport and warns about taxi and payment scams. Treat an unsolicited offer as something to verify, not a reason to rush.

Do not build the first evening around a reservation you cannot comfortably miss. Check in, find drinking water, eat somewhere manageable and take a short walk if you have energy. A delayed flight is much less disruptive when it only changes dinner rather than unravelling an expensive tour booking.

A practical three-day Hanoi itinerary

Day one: Hoan Kiem and a small part of the Old Quarter

Begin with a short walk around Hoan Kiem, then choose a few streets north of the lake rather than trying to “finish” the Old Quarter. The point is to get your bearings: learn the route back to your hotel, notice where you can sit down and identify a convenient place for a meal. Save a few pins, but leave room to change direction.

For food, choose one or two things you particularly want to try rather than arranging a checklist of dishes in a single sitting. Pho, bun cha, banh mi and egg coffee already feature in the site's Hanoi coverage. Portion size, ingredients and preparation vary between businesses; ask about anything that matters to you rather than assuming a familiar dish name guarantees the same meal everywhere.

Spend the afternoon on another short loop or take a proper break. A useful first evening can be as modest as dinner near your room and a return to the lake. If you want nightlife, decide beforehand how you will get back. Keep the next morning flexible instead of treating a late finish as a failure to follow the itinerary.

Day two: one major cultural visit, then a second neighbourhood

Choose a cultural anchor such as the Temple of Literature, Hoa Lo Prison or the Vietnamese Women’s Museum. Check the institution’s current visitor information for opening arrangements and tickets. Give the visit enough time to read and reflect, rather than using a museum as a brief stop between meals and photo locations.

Afterwards, choose a nearby lunch and assess how you feel before adding another attraction. Two places visited attentively can make a more memorable day than five rushed entrances. If you are travelling with someone whose interests differ from yours, agree which stop matters to each person and which one you will drop if the day becomes too tiring.

Later, consider Truc Bach or a manageable section of West Lake. Vietnam Tourism’s Hanoi walking routes show how several central streets and lake areas can be combined. Use them as route ideas and check conditions on the ground. There is no need to follow a published walk exactly when heat, rain, roadworks or your mobility make another route more sensible.

Day three: food, a lesson or time to revisit something

Keep your third day for the interest that emerged during the first two. That might be a food tour, another museum, a temple visit, a language lesson or a longer café break. A guide is useful when you want interpretation and help asking questions. Before booking, ask what the experience includes and whether its pace and food choices suit you.

The existing site includes Carl’s lesson with Quynh and a Vietnamese lesson testimonial. Watch that material if a lesson interests you, then contact the provider for current availability and pricing. A recorded experience is useful context; it should not be read as a promise that the same schedule or rate is still offered.

Leave a margin before your onward journey. Collecting luggage, finding transport and dealing with an unexpected delay all take time. Avoid fitting a distant attraction between checkout and an important departure simply because the route planner shows a short journey under ideal conditions.

How to turn three days into a week

Use the extra time to slow the city portion down and choose one excursion, rather than adding several long transfers. Four nights in Hanoi plus an overnight bay trip can be a workable shape for some travellers, but flight times and personal pace should determine the final arrangement. You do not need to spend every available day away from the city to justify a week there.

The site’s Ha Long Bay guide is the starting point for comparing a day trip with an overnight cruise. Ask about the actual embarkation location, transfer duration, cabin category, included activities and weather cancellation terms. A photograph of the bay does not tell you how much of the booked day will be spent on the road.

For a land-based alternative, read the Ninh Binh guide and compare the activities you want with the transport required. Choose an excursion because it fits your interests and physical comfort. A package advertised as “all the highlights” can be poor value if it spends most of the day moving you between things you never particularly wanted to do.

Keep a final night with a sensible connection to your departure if the consequences of a delayed return would be expensive. Ask operators about changes caused by weather and maintain a city-based fallback. Avoid treating a boat trip as certain until you understand the actual operating and cancellation conditions.

Train Street is optional; the railway is not a viewing platform

Do not enter railway tracks, cross barriers, disregard instructions from authorities or rely on a café purchase as permission to be beside an operating train. An old video shows a moment that happened; it does not establish present access, permission or safety. This itinerary works without Train Street, and no photograph is worth forcing entry into a restricted area.

The previous article included fixed train-time suggestions, advice to wait at a trackside café and language suggesting that buying a drink settled access. Those recommendations have been removed. If you are curious about Carl’s earlier footage, use the linked film as historical context and choose a different activity when access is restricted or unclear.

There are plenty of ways to photograph urban life without arranging a close encounter with a train. Work from an authorised public position, keep out of traffic, avoid blocking entrances and ask before making a person the subject of a close portrait. A quieter frame can tell more about a place than an unsafe stunt.

Eating well when your choices need more care

If you have dietary restrictions, prepare a clear explanation in Vietnamese and check that it describes the ingredients or preparation that matter to you. A dish name alone is not enough to establish what is in the kitchen's version. For a serious allergy, get individual medical advice before travel and discuss your needs directly with the business rather than relying on a tour label such as “vegetarian-friendly.”

Before booking a food walk, ask whether substitutions are possible, whether you can skip a stop and how the guide handles uncertainty about ingredients. Keep an alternative meal option near your accommodation so that a cancelled reservation or an unsuitable menu does not leave you searching while tired. If food is the main reason for your visit, spread the experiences across several days; you will have more appetite and more time to understand what you are trying.

Build a budget from bookings you can actually make

Start with the room price for your travel dates, including taxes and the cancellation policy, then add airport transport, food, local journeys and the activities you genuinely intend to book. Keep international flights and excursions separate from ordinary city spending. Otherwise, one cruise or long transfer can make a “daily budget” misleading.

This guide does not present old taxi quotes or restaurant prices as current guarantees. Save dated quotes for the items that will dominate your spending, and note what each includes. Two hotel prices are not comparable if one includes breakfast and airport pickup while the other is for a different room category with no flexibility.

Decide where paying more will improve your trip. A quieter room, a lift, a convenient location or a better-timed transfer may matter more than an extra attraction. Conversely, a premium hotel across town may cost you time every day if your interests are concentrated around the Old Quarter. Make the budget serve your priorities rather than a target copied from somebody else's trip.

Carry a practical mix of payment options and enough small cash for purchases where cards are not accepted. Check a price before ordering or agreeing to a service, particularly when the unit is unclear: per person, per ride, per hour or for the whole group. Keep a separate reserve for an unexpected transport or accommodation change.

Walking and local transport without wishful thinking

Group nearby activities and use a vehicle for journeys that would otherwise consume your energy. A map distance does not describe the quality of a footpath, the number of crossings or the comfort of walking with luggage. Evaluate the route itself, especially with children, mobility aids or a heavy camera bag.

Use crossings and signals where available, wait for a safe opportunity and remain attentive to vehicles. Do not step into moving traffic on the assumption that everyone will swerve around you. The old guide described that as a reliable technique; it was too confident and has been removed. If a crossing feels unsafe, find another place to cross or take a different route.

Before accepting a ride, match the vehicle and driver to the booking and confirm the destination. Keep the route accessible without holding your phone towards passing traffic. For more examples of payment and street-service problems, see the Old Quarter scams guide. A clear agreement at the start usually gives you a better basis for resolving a misunderstanding.

Give weather, air quality and rest a place in the plan

Check the short-range weather forecast close to departure and again before an excursion. Build a day with places to pause indoors, rather than an uninterrupted outdoor schedule. Pack for the conditions forecast for your actual dates and remember that a light bag is only useful if it still contains what keeps you comfortable.

Air quality deserves attention as well as temperature. Smartraveller notes that pollution can be high in major Vietnamese cities. If you have a condition affected by air quality, discuss your travel plan with your clinician and use current local information when deciding how much time to spend outdoors. An attractive lake view is not evidence that the surrounding air is clean.

Use a wet afternoon for a museum, a lesson, an indoor meal or a rest. If a pool is part of your plan, check the Hanoi pools article, then confirm current guest access and conditions directly. Do not assume every hotel with a pool sells day access, or that last season's advertised arrangement still applies.

Ask concrete access questions before booking

“Accessible” and “easy walking” are not precise enough to plan from. Ask accommodation providers about steps between the street and reception, lift access to your particular room, bathroom arrangements and the narrowest doorway that matters for your equipment. Request recent photographs if the answer is unclear. A lift in the building does not necessarily eliminate every step.

For tours, ask about vehicle entry, how long you will stand, available toilets and whether a shorter route is possible. For boats, ask about the actual boarding arrangement and cabin access. Keep the answers in writing so that your needs are understood before the day begins.

If noise or crowds are difficult, shorten your outings and make returning to your room easy. Travelling at a different pace does not make a visit less worthwhile. A well-chosen morning and a comfortable afternoon can be more successful than a full-day itinerary built around somebody else's endurance.

Filming, photographs and being a reasonable guest

Carry the equipment you will use that day and leave the rest securely stored. Changing lenses or checking footage is easier somewhere you can stop without blocking a walkway. Ask before close filming, respect a refusal and follow the rules displayed by museums, temples and private businesses.

For drone use, professional filming or sensitive locations, verify the applicable permission requirements with the relevant authority or venue before recording. Ownership of a camera or drone does not itself grant permission. Carl’s Hanoi by Night page offers film context, while the filmmaking guide can help you think about how much equipment a scene needs.

A visit you would actually want to repeat

A good Hanoi plan leaves enough space to notice what interests you. Start with a convenient base, give the city a few days, choose one longer excursion if you have time and keep the last connection realistic. If a place is closed, a walk feels unsafe or you are simply tired, changing the plan is a sensible response.

About the author

Carl Tomich is an Australian documentary filmmaker and travel creator who has filmed and lived across Vietnam, Japan, Bali, Thailand, Albania, Montenegro, Croatia, and Australia. His guides come from being on the ground with camera gear, local transport, short-term rentals, visa runs, long travel days, and the practical mistakes that rarely make it into polished tourism content.
