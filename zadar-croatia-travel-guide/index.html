Northern Dalmatia · Croatia travel guide

Zadar, Croatia Travel Guide

Old Town walks, the Sea Organ, Adriatic beaches and day trips that fit the time you have.

Destinations / Zadar

By Carl Tomich · Checked 27 October 2026 · Editorial policy

Zadar: start with the peninsula

You can see Zadar’s main sights on foot without spending the day moving between attractions. Roman remains, medieval churches, Venetian walls and the modern Sea Organ sit close together on the Old Town peninsula. Give the waterfront time as well as the buildings: the sound of the waves and the changing evening light are part of the visit.

Zadar is in northern Dalmatia on Croatia’s Adriatic coast. The peninsula is its historic centre, connected to a much larger modern city by the landward approach and a pedestrian bridge across the harbour. The main bus station, many residential neighbourhoods and several beaches are outside the Old Town. “Zadar accommodation” does not necessarily mean walking distance from the Forum.

One full day covers the historic centre; two or three days leave space for a beach, an island or one regional excursion. The video below introduces the Old Town, Sea Organ and sunset. The guide adds researched transport and visiting information to help you plan beyond the film.

A short history you can see in the streets

Roman Iader developed on an earlier Liburnian settlement. Its ordered street plan and Forum helped establish the shape of the historic centre. Stand beside the columns and fragments around St Donatus and you can read that older city beneath the later buildings.

The ninth-century Church of St Donatus belongs to the early medieval city. Nearby St Anastasia’s Cathedral has earlier Christian roots, while much of the building visitors see dates from the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The buildings make more sense together than as isolated photo stops.

Venetian rule was contested over centuries; the long later period ran from 1409 to 1797. The Land Gate and bastions express Zadar’s importance as an Adriatic stronghold. Its defensive system is one component of the UNESCO-listed Venetian Works of Defence, inscribed in 2017. The designation concerns the fortifications, rather than every building in the Old Town.

Austrian and French administrations followed Venice. Zadar became an Italian enclave under the 1920 Treaty of Rapallo, suffered extensive bombing in 1943–44, and was rebuilt within post-war Yugoslavia. It is now part of independent Croatia. This history helps explain the mixture of old monuments, open spaces and newer buildings within the peninsula.

Things to do in Zadar’s Old Town

Sea Organ and Greeting to the Sun

At the northwestern tip of the peninsula, Nikola Bašić’s Sea Organ turns wave movement into sound through pipes beneath the waterfront steps. Sit and listen: the music changes with the sea. Beside it, Greeting to the Sun uses solar energy for a light display after dark. Both are outdoor public installations with no sightseeing admission ticket.

The area gets busy around sunset. Arrive before the last light, leave room for others and be careful beside the water in wind or swell. The wider Riva promenade gives you other places to watch the sky if the steps are crowded.

Roman Forum

The Forum is an open-air public space beside St Donatus. Look for the surviving columns, paving and scattered architectural fragments, then compare them with the church built alongside them. Walking through the square is free; neighbouring museums and interiors have their own admission arrangements. Avoid climbing on ancient stonework.

Church of St Donatus

The rounded ninth-century church is one of Zadar’s most recognisable buildings, with a spare interior and a long association with concerts. Viewing it from the Forum is free. Interior sightseeing is ticketed when operating; concerts may have separate tickets.

Check the Archaeological Museum’s current visiting information before planning an interior visit. Its published schedule varies by month, with shorter autumn hours and winter restrictions; the museum also announced restoration funding in 2026. That announcement does not establish a permanent closure, but it makes checking current access especially useful.

St Anastasia’s Cathedral

Walk around to the Romanesque façade, then visit the cathedral when it is open outside services. It remains a place of worship. Sightseeing admission, service access and the separate bell-tower climb should not be treated as one ticket. Check the current arrangements at the entrance; the tower is an optional stair climb for city views, not an essential part of the walk.

People’s Square and Five Wells Square

People’s Square, or Narodni trg, is a good pause between the landward entrance and the cathedral area. Its civic buildings and café terraces show a different side of the city from the Roman monuments. The square is free to walk through; exhibitions inside surrounding buildings may charge.

Five Wells Square, Trg pet bunara, preserves the line of wellheads associated with the city’s sixteenth-century water supply. Pair it with the adjoining park and the nearby gate rather than making a separate journey. The square itself is public and free.

Land Gate, Foša and the Venetian walls

The Land Gate at Foša dates to 1543. Look up at its carved decoration and the Venetian lion, then step back beside the small harbour to see how the gate, water and defences fit together. Continue along publicly accessible sections of the walls and their surroundings. This is a different experience from buying a ticket for a continuous circuit of city walls: do not assume every rampart is open or connected.

A practical Old Town walking route

  1. Foša and the Land Gate: begin on the landward side of the peninsula, looking back across the little harbour before entering.
  2. Five Wells Square: pause by the wellheads; add the neighbouring park if you want shade and a slower start.
  3. People’s Square: continue to Narodni trg, with a café stop if you have time.
  4. Kalelarga: follow the main pedestrian street toward the cathedral and Forum area.
  5. St Anastasia, St Donatus and the Forum: visit interiors according to their current hours, then explore the outdoor remains.
  6. Sea Organ and Greeting to the Sun: finish at the tip of the peninsula, ideally returning for sunset and the light display after dark.
  7. Riva return: walk back along the seafront toward the university and Foša, or cut through the centre to your accommodation.

Allow two to three hours for an unhurried outdoor walk with pauses, longer for museums, church interiors or a meal. This is a suggested itinerary, not a timed guided tour. In summer, do the exposed sections early or late and keep midday for lunch and an interior visit. Stone paving, steps and uneven edges mean this is not a verified step-free route.

Beaches and swimming

Kolovare: easiest from the Old Town

Kolovare lies southeast of the peninsula, roughly a 15–25-minute walk from the Land Gate depending on which section you choose. Expect pebbles, hard bathing platforms and stony entries rather than a broad sandy resort beach. The area has established bathing facilities, including showers and changing areas, with places for refreshments nearby; seasonal services should be checked on arrival.

It works well for a swim around a city day. Bring water shoes if you dislike pebbles, and check the entry before stepping into the sea. For more coastline, continue toward Karma and rocky Punta Bajlo rather than expecting an identical beach all the way.

Borik and Diklo: more of a beach stay

Borik is several kilometres northwest of the Old Town, roughly 4 km by road depending on your starting point. A local bus or taxi is usually more practical than a hot cross-city walk. The beach area mixes pebble and sandy sections, with beach bars and seasonal recreation. Resort services, loungers and activities may cost extra: check the particular entrance and facilities rather than assuming everything is included.

Farther along the coast, Diklo offers a mixture of shoreline bathing areas and accommodation. It suits visitors happy to travel into the centre for sightseeing. For a quieter-feeling coastal outing, Petrčane is another option outside the city, requiring a separate bus or drive.

Choose a designated bathing area and follow local flags and signs. Do not assume the Sea Organ steps are a supervised beach. Wind, water temperature and sea conditions matter more than a promised swimming season; spring or autumn sunshine does not guarantee comfortable water.

Getting to Zadar

From Zadar Airport

Use the airport’s official ground-transport page for the timetable that matches your travel month. The Liburnija airport service links the airport, Gaženica ferry port, the main bus station and the peninsula. The airport publishes an approximate 20-minute journey to the centre, but allow extra time for traffic, stops and waiting.

The published one-way fare is €5 including luggage, checked on 27 October 2026. Confirm the latest fare and stop at the airport before boarding. For an early departure or a late arrival outside the bus schedule, arrange a taxi or transfer and agree the pickup and price in advance.

Flight routes and frequencies vary substantially by season. Check the airport and airline for your dates rather than relying on a summer route list for winter travel.

Intercity bus

Zadar’s main bus station is on Ante Starčevića, outside the historic centre. Allow roughly 20–30 minutes on foot to the landward end of the Old Town, longer with luggage or summer heat. Local buses or taxis can cover the final section. Check your accommodation’s actual entrance before dragging a suitcase across the peninsula.

Operators sell journeys linking Zadar with other Croatian cities, including Zagreb and Split. Compare the exact service: motorway and coastal routes have different durations, luggage conditions and stops. Reserve busy departures and leave a buffer before a flight or ferry connection.

Driving and arriving by sea

Zadar is reached from the A1 motorway and regional coastal roads. Check the route and tolls for your vehicle, and expect slower approaches during summer changeover days. Arrange parking before entering the centre; a room in a pedestrian lane may require a separate luggage drop-off.

For islands, distinguish Zadar’s city passenger port from Gaženica outside the Old Town. Jadrolinija’s line 409 connects the city port with Preko for foot passengers; vehicle ferry line 431 uses Gaženica. Use the operator’s current port and sailing details. International and other seasonal sailings are not assumed in this guide.

Getting around and whether to rent a car

Walk within the Old Town. Its main sights are close together, and repeated taxi rides add little to a peninsula visit. Liburnija’s city buses are useful for the bus station and areas such as Borik and Diklo. Check the stop, direction and current timetable; evening and weekend frequencies can differ from weekday daytime service.

Use a taxi for luggage, a late arrival or a beach transfer when the bus timing does not suit. Request the fare or check the app quote before setting off. For parking, read the sign at the actual space: public zones, private garages and seasonal tariffs differ. A paid space is not automatically valid for unlimited hours.

A rental car is useful for combining mainland stops or choosing your own start at Paklenica, Krka or Plitvice. It is usually unnecessary for a short city stay or a simple Preko outing on foot. Consider renting only for the excursion day, then compare the total with a tour: include parking, fuel, tolls and park tickets.

Where to stay: choose for the trip you want

  • Old Town: best for walking to sights, restaurants and the evening waterfront. Expect pedestrian access, possible stairs and noise near busy lanes. Confirm air-conditioning, lift access and the distance from legal parking or a taxi drop-off.
  • Kolovare and the southeastern side: a useful compromise for beach access, the bus station and walking into the centre. Exact location matters: compare the route to the Land Gate, not just a broad neighbourhood label. Ask whether parking is reserved or simply nearby.
  • Borik and Diklo: better suited to a beach-focused stay, often with apartments or resort-style facilities. You trade immediate Old Town access for the coast. Check the bus stop, evening return options, beach surface and whether advertised parking is on-site.

These are area comparisons, not hotel endorsements. Before booking, compare the full price, cancellation terms, tourist tax, parking and any cleaning charge. In winter, confirm heating and which nearby places are operating.

Food, drink and the maraschino tradition

Look for Dalmatian seafood dishes such as grilled fish, black cuttlefish risotto, mussels prepared na buzaru, squid and brudet fish stew. Olive oil, seasonal vegetables and bread often accompany the meal. Regional cheese and cured meats provide other options, while bakeries and markets can make a simpler breakfast or lunch practical.

Ask how fish is priced before ordering: a menu price by weight can be very different from a price per portion. Confirm whether sides are included, and ask about ingredients if you need vegetarian food or have an allergy. Dishes prepared slowly or for a group may need advance ordering. Reserve a popular dinner spot in peak season, but do not assume every summer restaurant keeps winter hours.

Zadar’s maraschino is a clear liqueur made from marasca cherries, linked to the city’s long distilling tradition. It is a local drink to ask about or take home, rather than a name for the bright cocktail cherries alone. No restaurant visit or meal here is presented as a personal recommendation from the film.

Day trips: islands, small towns and national parks

The options below use very different amounts of time. Driving estimates are one way from Zadar in ordinary conditions, excluding parking, queues and sightseeing. Public transport can take longer and needs a workable return service.

Ugljan: the straightforward island outing

Take the passenger boat from the city port to Preko, then explore on foot or plan a local island-bus connection. Jadrolinija’s 2026 line 409 schedule gives a 25-minute crossing. This is scheduled public transport, not an island-hopping sightseeing tour. Check the return sailing before leaving the harbour. If taking a vehicle, use the separate Gaženica–Preko ferry and include the journey to Gaženica in your plan.

Nin: an easier half day

Nin is around 15 km from Zadar, with a compact historic centre on a small islet and sandy lagoon beaches nearby. Allow around half an hour by car, subject to traffic. Liburnija publishes a Nin–Zaton suburban route; check both directions and the day of the week. It can be a half-day cultural outing, or a longer trip if you add the beach or saltworks visitor activities.

Paklenica National Park: choose a hike first

The main Velika Paklenica entrance is near Starigrad-Paklenica. Allow roughly 45–60 minutes by road. A bus to Starigrad does not necessarily drop you at the park gate, so plan the final approach and return. Choose a trail appropriate to your fitness: a canyon walk and a high mountain route are different days. Carry suitable shoes, water and weather protection, and check the park’s current access information.

Krka National Park: plan the entrance

For Skradinski buk, choose Skradin or Lozovac before booking transport. Allow about 1–1½ hours by car to the entrance area, then extra time for the park’s internal access and walking. Public buses need careful checking for the actual park gateway and return; an organised excursion can simplify those connections. Treat Krka as the main outing of the day, and follow the park’s current trail and swimming restrictions.

Plitvice Lakes: a substantial full day

Allow roughly two hours by car each way, with more time for traffic and stops. Bus services can take around two to three hours each way; verify the exact date and entrance served. Book a timed park ticket and allow several hours inside. A late outward bus and an early return will not provide a useful visit. An overnight near the park is worth considering if you want a less compressed day.

Kornati: an organised day on the water

Kornati excursions are generally full-day boat trips run by operators, rather than public ferries you can use like the Preko crossing. The national park lists contracted excursion providers. Compare the actual route, boat size, time ashore, shade, meals and whether park admission is included. “Kornati” marketing can cover itineraries with different stops, including Telašćica, which is a separate protected area.

Book popular departures ahead, confirm the boarding port and ask about weather cancellation terms. Strong wind can change or cancel a trip; keep a mainland alternative rather than scheduling a boat tour as the only possible activity before your flight.

Best time to visit

Summer

Summer offers the widest choice of flights, boat excursions and beach services, alongside the greatest heat and crowds. Start sightseeing early, carry water and use the middle of the day for shade, lunch or an interior visit. Reserve accommodation and popular trips ahead.

Spring and autumn

These seasons can work well for walking and a slower city break. Bring a layer and rain protection, check the shorter attraction hours and confirm the excursion you want is actually running. A warm afternoon does not guarantee warm water or a calm crossing.

Winter

The city remains a working regional centre, but visitor services are reduced: some restaurants close, flight choices narrow and boat tours are less dependable. Outdoor sights remain worthwhile in suitable weather. Plan around current transport and museum information rather than copying a summer itinerary.

One day or two to three days

One full day in Zadar

Begin at Foša and the Land Gate, then walk through Five Wells Square and People’s Square toward Kalelarga. Spend late morning around the Forum, St Donatus and St Anastasia, choosing any interiors that are open. Have lunch and a break; in hot weather, keep the afternoon flexible. Return to the Riva and Sea Organ before sunset, then see Greeting to the Sun after dark. A quick Kolovare swim is optional if conditions and time allow, rather than another fixed obligation.

Two days

Use the first day for the Old Town. On the second, choose Preko on Ugljan or a beach-focused day around Kolovare or Borik. This gives you a second chance at the sunset if the first evening was cloudy, without organising both days around long-distance travel.

Three days

Keep one day for the city, one for an island or beach, and one for a selected mainland park or a full-day Kornati excursion. Choose the park according to your interests and transport, rather than trying to combine Paklenica, Krka and Plitvice in a single day. For several regional trips, add nights so Zadar remains a place you see, not just somewhere you sleep.

Indicative budget in euros

Prices below were checked on 27 October 2026 and are published reference points, not prices paid during the video. Check your date and ticket category before booking.

  • Outdoor Old Town sights: no admission ticket for the Sea Organ, Greeting to the Sun, Forum, People’s Square, Five Wells Square or viewing the Land Gate. Interiors, exhibitions and concerts can cost extra.
  • Airport bus: €5 one way including luggage, as listed by Zadar Airport.
  • Garage Center: the operator lists €1–3 per started hour across its winter, spring and summer tariffs. This is one garage, not a citywide parking price.
  • Krka: standard individual adult admission for all accessible land sites is €7 in January–March and November–December, €20 in April–May and October, and €40 in June–September on the 2026 tariff. Check transport and excursion inclusions.
  • Plitvice: standard adult day entry is €10 in January–March and November–December, €23 in April–May and October, and €40 for the main daytime entry period in June–September. Summer late-entry tickets are €25, with the cutoff varying by month; do not choose late admission without checking the remaining visiting time.

Accommodation and meal costs depend heavily on dates, location and what is included. No reliable general range was established for this guide, so room, restaurant and total daily figures are omitted. Compare date-specific room totals and actual menus; allow separately for parking, transfers and excursions. A mostly outdoor city day has a very different cost from a park or boat-tour day.

Practical tips

  • Footwear: polished stone can be slippery, especially when wet. Wear shoes with grip; beach shoes help on stony sea entries.
  • Sun and water: the Forum and waterfront have exposed sections. Carry drinking water, sunscreen and a hat, and plan breaks in summer.
  • Timetables: save the current return bus or ferry and the correct port before setting out. Seasonal timetables, weekends and holidays can change the options.
  • Bookings: reserve popular park entry slots and boat excursions, and check cancellation terms. Confirm what a tour price includes.
  • Churches and residents: dress appropriately inside churches, give worship priority and keep late-night noise down in residential lanes. Avoid blocking doors while taking photographs.
  • Access: ask about stairs and luggage access before booking an Old Town room. Check specific routes and facilities if you need step-free travel.
  • Payments: plan in euros, carry a small cash reserve and confirm card acceptance for smaller purchases or transport. Save tickets and booking details offline.

Frequently asked questions

Is Zadar walkable?

The Old Town is compact and best explored on foot. The main bus station and beach districts are farther out; check the actual address rather than assuming all of Zadar is one short walk.

Do you need a car?

No for the Old Town, a city beach stay with bus access or a foot-passenger trip to Preko. A car becomes useful for independent mainland excursions, but adds parking costs during a city stay.

How many days should you stay?

One full day covers the historic centre. Two or three days gives you time for the coast and one excursion. Add more nights if several parks and islands are priorities.

Does Zadar work as a regional base?

Yes, if you choose trips selectively. Ugljan and Nin are relatively simple outings, while Plitvice and Kornati each use a substantial day. Check connections before assuming you can return from every destination late in the evening.

Are the Sea Organ and Greeting to the Sun free?

Yes. They are public waterfront installations. Sunset is popular; stay for darkness to see the light installation, and take care near the water.

Which port do I need for Ugljan?

For the line 409 passenger boat to Preko, use the city port. For the line 431 vehicle ferry, use Gaženica. Always recheck your ticket and the operator’s current port notice.

Can you visit in winter?

Yes for a city break with current opening and transport checks. Expect fewer seasonal flights, tours and beach services, and plan flexible outdoor time around the weather.
